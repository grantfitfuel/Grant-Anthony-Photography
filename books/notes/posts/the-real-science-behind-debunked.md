Title: The real science behind Debunked
Date: 2026-10-30
Book: debunked
Summary: A Princeton lab that tried to move machines with the mind. A worldwide network of random number generators. A US Army unit paid to see at a distance. All of it real.

Callum Ferris, the narrator of *Debunked*, makes his living taking apart claims of the paranormal. He has a spreadsheet. It has colour-coded tabs. So when I gave him something to investigate, I wanted the strangest parts to be the parts he couldn’t wave away, because they actually happened.

Here are three of them.

## The Princeton lab

In 1979 Robert Jahn, then Dean of Engineering at Princeton University, set up the Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research lab, known as PEAR. Its question was simple and slightly mad: can a person’s intention change the behaviour of a machine?

Most of the experiments used random event generators, electronic devices that produce an unpredictable stream of ones and zeros, a coin toss done by circuitry. Volunteers sat in front of them and tried, by intention alone, to push the results high or low. Over millions of trials the lab reported a tiny effect, roughly one extra head in every thousand tosses.

Tiny, but if it were real it would matter enormously. The trouble is that it didn’t hold up. A large attempt to repeat the results with two German research groups, published in 2000, failed to reproduce them, and critics noted that one regular participant, thought to be on the staff, accounted for about half of the overall effect. The lab closed in February 2007, after nearly thirty years.

## The eggs

One of the PEAR researchers, Roger Nelson, took the idea worldwide. In August 1998 the Global Consciousness Project began collecting data from random number generators, which the team called eggs, running in different countries. By 2004 there were between sixty-five and seventy of them, from Fiji to South Africa.

The idea was that when huge numbers of people are pulled in the same direction at once, by a disaster or a celebration, the eggs might drift away from randomness together. The project registered its predictions before looking at the data, and across hundreds of events it reported odds against chance of more than a trillion to one.

Its most famous result comes from 11 September 2001, when the network showed a large deviation around the time of the attacks. Critics replied that choosing a slightly shorter or longer stretch of time would have made it vanish.

## The government psychics

From the 1970s until 1995, the United States paid people to see things at a distance. It began with research at the Stanford Research Institute in California and grew into a small Army intelligence unit at Fort Meade in Maryland, later known as the Stargate Project. Its remote viewers were given a target, sometimes no more than a set of map coordinates, and asked to describe what was there.

In 1995 an outside review was ordered. Its two experts disagreed. The statistician Jessica Utts concluded that something real was happening in the laboratory. The psychologist Ray Hyman said that hadn’t been shown. The review’s verdict was the one that mattered: remote viewing had never been shown to be of use in intelligence work. The programme closed that year, and in 2017 the CIA put its files online.

## Where the book comes in

*Debunked* invents the people and the danger. Not the research. Callum starts where any sceptic would, with studies like these and the holes in them, and he’d be the first to point the holes out.

Then his grandmother, an hour before she dies, opens her eyes and says something she had no way of knowing.

> “There’s your grandfather,” she said. “In his good jumper.”

And then the deaths start: fringe consciousness researchers, one after another. A sceptic who can’t explain something has a problem. He has to keep looking.
