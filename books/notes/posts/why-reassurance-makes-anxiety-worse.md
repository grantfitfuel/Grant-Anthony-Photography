Title: Why reassurance makes anxiety worse
Date: 2026-10-30
Book: the-calibrated-mind
Summary: Checking, asking, searching your symptoms online. Reassurance brings relief within minutes, and that relief is exactly what keeps the fear in place. Why it works that way, and how to step off.

It’s late and you notice it again: a flutter in your chest, a skipped beat, maybe nothing. You pick up your phone and search. The first page you find says it’s usually harmless. Relief, real relief, spreading through you. Then, twenty minutes later, the word *usually* starts to bother you. What about the times it isn’t? You read another page. You ask your partner, “Do you think it’s fine?” They say it’s fine. Relief again.

Tomorrow you’ll do it all again.

If you live with anxiety, you probably know this loop, whatever shape it takes for you. Checking the door is locked, then checking again. Asking a friend if that message sounded rude. Re-reading an email you’ve already sent. Going over a symptom, a worry, a thought, until someone or something tells you it’s all right. And underneath it is the question *The Calibrated Mind* opens with, the one almost everyone with anxiety arrives at sooner or later:

> If I know my fear isn’t rational, why does it still feel so real?

Part of the answer is that the very thing you do to feel better is teaching your brain to keep the fear.

## The relief is real. So is the cost.

Reassurance works. That’s the problem. It works fast, and it feels good, and anything that makes a horrible feeling stop is something your brain will want to do again. Every time checking brings relief, the urge to check gets a little stronger.

But the relief doesn’t last. It never quite settles the question, because no answer can promise you that nothing bad will ever happen, and that’s the promise anxiety is really asking for. So the doubt comes back, often within minutes, and you need another dose. Most people who’ve lived with this notice the same thing: each dose seems to last a little less long than the one before.

## What your brain learns

Your brain is a prediction machine. It’s constantly forecasting what’s about to happen, and its forecasts of danger are what you feel as anxiety. When it predicts a threat, your body gets ready for it. The racing heart, the tight chest, the urge to do something: those are real, and they’re your body doing exactly what it was asked to do.

A prediction changes by being tested and turning out wrong. If your brain predicts that something terrible will happen and then it doesn’t, the prediction gets a little weaker. That’s how fear fades. It isn’t argued away. It’s disproved, by experience, over and over.

Reassurance interrupts the test. You feel the fear, you check, and the fear goes. But from your brain’s point of view, the lesson isn’t “there was no danger”. The lesson is “there was danger, and checking saved me”. The prediction never gets the chance to be wrong, so it never gets weaker. You’ve kept it safe.

The book uses a smoke alarm to explain it. The alarm isn’t broken. It goes off when the toast browns because that’s what alarms do. What has moved is the threshold.

Reassurance is like fanning the alarm every time it goes off. It goes quiet. It also stays exactly as sensitive as it was.

## The quiet ways we protect ourselves

Not all reassurance looks like asking. Some of it is so small you don’t notice you’re doing it.

Someone who has panic attacks might sit down at the first sign of dizziness, in case they faint. They never faint, and they put that down to sitting. Someone anxious in company might rehearse every sentence before saying it, avoid eye contact, keep a drink in their hand to stop it shaking. Someone with health anxiety might press on the same spot every morning to see if it still hurts. All of these feel like sensible precautions. All of them stop the fear from being tested.

They have a name, safety behaviours, and there’s good evidence of what they cost. In a study published in 1999, people with panic disorder and agoraphobia were exposed to the situations they feared for just fifteen minutes. Some were asked to drop their safety behaviours while they did it, and some were asked to keep them. The ones who dropped them showed a significantly bigger fall in both their anxiety and their belief that something catastrophic would happen. Same situation, same fifteen minutes. The only difference was whether the fear got a fair test.

## Stepping off the loop

None of this means you should white-knuckle it alone. It means the way out runs through the discomfort, not around it, and there are kinder and harder ways to do that.

The kindest place to start is delay. When the urge to check arrives, don’t forbid it. Just put it off. Ten minutes. Notice what the anxiety does while you wait: it rises, often sharply, and then, if you leave it alone, it starts to come down on its own. That curve is the most important thing you can see for yourself, because it’s the test your brain has been missing. Next time, wait a little longer.

It helps to name the prediction. “If I don’t check the door again, someone will break in.” “If I don’t ask her, it means she’s angry with me.” Saying it plainly does two things. It turns a vague dread into something specific enough to test. And it often shows you how much the anxiety is asking of you.

If other people are part of your reassurance, bring them in. Agree with them, at a calm moment, that when you ask the same question again they’ll answer kindly but only once, or say something like “I think that’s the anxiety asking.” It feels unkind at first, to both of you. It’s the opposite.

Then work through the quieter safety behaviours, one at a time. Stay standing for a minute when you feel dizzy. Say the sentence without rehearsing it. Leave the spot alone for a day. Each one is a small experiment, and each one gives your brain a piece of evidence it couldn’t get any other way.

## What it will feel like

It will feel worse before it feels better. That’s not a sign it isn’t working. It’s the sign it is: the prediction is being tested, and the first few tests are uncomfortable because your brain still believes the danger is real.

What usually happens, given time and repetition, is that the peaks get lower and come down faster, and the urge to check loosens its grip. It doesn’t vanish all at once. It gets recalibrated.

For OCD, panic and health anxiety in particular, this is the heart of the main psychological treatments, and a good therapist can make it far more manageable than doing it alone. If it’s taking over your days, speak to your GP about talking therapy.

One more thing, because it matters. None of this is about ignoring your body. A new symptom, or one that’s changing, deserves a proper look from your GP. This is about what happens after that: when you’ve been checked, and told you’re fine, and still can’t let it go.

## You’re not broken

*The Calibrated Mind* is built on one idea: anxiety isn’t a fault in you. It’s a prediction system that has been tuned towards danger. Or, as the book says:

> Anxiety is not a failure of logic. It is what happens when the predictions have been tuned toward danger. And a system that has been tuned can be tuned again.

You are not broken. You are miscalibrated.
