Music and gigs

Dim, coloured, ever-changing light and performers who never stand still. The settings, timing and manners that make gig photography work.

A gig is one of the hardest places to take a photograph. The light is dim, coloured and changes every second, the performers never stop moving, and you are often squeezed into a crowd. It is also one of the most rewarding, because a good gig photograph holds the noise, sweat and energy of a night in one frame.

This lesson covers the settings that work, how to handle stage lighting, how to time the moment, and how to behave in a venue so you are welcome back.

Starting settings

  • Mode: Manual with auto ISO, or Aperture priority with a minimum shutter speed set, so the camera never drops to a speed that blurs the band
  • Aperture: f/1.8 to f/2.8, wide open or close to it, for as much light as possible
  • Shutter speed: 1/250 s for a moving singer or guitarist; 1/500 s or faster for jumps and drummers; 1/125 s for a slow ballad
  • ISO: 1600 to 6400, higher if needed. A sharp, noisy frame beats a clean, blurred one
  • Metering: Spot or centre-weighted on the performer's face, because bright lights and black backgrounds fool the camera's general metering
  • Focus: Continuous autofocus with face or eye detection, or a single small point on the performer
  • Drive: Continuous, in short bursts of three or four frames
  • File format: RAW, for the room it gives with noise and coloured light

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

Lenses

Fast lenses matter more here than almost anywhere. A lens at f/2.8 lets in twice the light of one at f/4, which is one stop of ISO you do not have to use.

  • A 50 mm f/1.8: small, cheap and very bright. Good from the crowd at small venues
  • A 24-70 mm f/2.8: the standard pit lens, wide enough for the whole stage and long enough for one performer
  • A 70-200 mm f/2.8: for larger stages, or for working from further back
  • A wide lens, 16 mm to 35 mm: for the crowd and the scale of a big stage

Stage lighting

Stage light is the main challenge. It changes colour and brightness constantly, and LED lights are often deep red, blue or purple.

  • Wait for the light. It cycles. Watch a song for a minute, see when the performer's face is lit, and shoot then
  • Expose for the face, not the room. Most of the frame is black. Let it stay black
  • Watch red light. Saturated red and magenta can clip a single colour channel and lose all detail in skin. Underexpose by a third to two thirds of a stop under strong red
  • Use backlight. Lights behind the band, and haze from a smoke machine, give silhouettes and beams that look great with the right exposure
  • White balance: a fixed setting such as 3200 K to 4000 K is a sensible start; then correct from RAW. Auto changes every frame

No flash

Most venues and artists do not allow flash, and at a big show it would flatten the atmosphere if they did. Turn it off before you go in unless you have been told it is welcome. The stage light is the light.

If your camera has a silent electronic shutter, test it before relying on it. Under some LED stage lighting it records dark bands across the frame. The mechanical shutter avoids that.

When flash is allowed: rear sync and movement

At small gigs, club nights and DJ sets, the band or venue may be happy for you to use flash. Then rear curtain sync with a slow shutter is one of the most striking techniques there is. The flash freezes the performer sharp, while the slow shutter lets stage lights and movement trail behind them.

  • What rear sync does: normally the flash fires the moment the shutter opens, so trails from a moving subject land in front of it and look backwards. With rear curtain sync, also called second curtain sync, the flash fires just before the shutter closes, so the trails follow the movement naturally
  • Setting it: in the camera itself. Open the flash settings in the camera's menu and choose second curtain or rear curtain sync; on some cameras it is one of the flash modes. It works with a built-in flash or a flashgun on the hot shoe
  • Shutter speed: 1/4 s to 1/15 s. Slower gives longer trails; faster keeps more of the scene clean
  • Aperture and ISO: f/4 to f/8 and ISO 400 to 1600, so the stage lights record during the long exposure
  • Flash: automatic (TTL) with flash compensation of minus 1 to minus 2 stops, or manual at 1/16 to 1/32 power. Direct, or bounced off a low, pale ceiling
  • Adding movement: let the performer's movement make the trails, or move the camera during the exposure. A slight twist or a short pan turns stage lights into streaks around a sharp figure
  • Colour: an orange gel on the flash helps it blend with warm stage light instead of looking cold and white

Ask first, keep it to a few frames per song, and never fire flash straight into a performer's eyes from close range.

Timing the moment

Gig photographs are about peaks. Learn the songs if you can, and watch for:

  • The singer at the microphone, eyes open, mouth mid-note
  • Guitar solos, when the guitarist steps forward or leans back
  • Jumps and kicks, usually on a big chorus or the end of a song
  • The drummer, best caught between cymbal hits, from the side of the stage
  • Interaction: two band members together, a hand reaching to the crowd
  • The crowd itself: hands in the air against the lights, from the front or side

Shoot short bursts as a moment builds, not long ones. Keep the microphone stand out of the face where you can by moving a step left or right.

Rules, passes and permission

  • Small venues: ask the band or the venue first. Many are happy, especially if you share photographs afterwards
  • Larger venues: photographing from the pit usually needs a photo pass, arranged through the band's management, the promoter or a publication. Cameras with detachable lenses are often not allowed without one
  • The first three songs: at many larger gigs, pit photographers may only shoot for the first three songs, without flash. Check the rules with whoever gives you the pass
  • Using the photographs: a pass may come with conditions on where photographs can be published. Read it before you sign. Selling photographs of a performer usually needs their permission

Being a good gig photographer

  • Do not block the audience. They paid to see the band, not the back of your head. Keep the camera low and move on after a few frames
  • No camera held high over your head for long
  • In the pit, move with the other photographers, not against them, and duck when you pass in front of someone's lens
  • Keep your kit small. One body, one or two lenses, a small bag

Protect your hearing

Live music is often louder than 100 decibels near the stage, and hours at that level can damage hearing for good. Wear earplugs made for music. They lower the volume evenly, so the band still sounds right and you can still hear what is happening around you.

Editing gig photographs

  • Noise: reduce noise first, then judge the sharpening on the clean result; see Sharpening and noise
  • Colour: correct white balance to make skin believable, then reduce saturation in any channel that has gone solid, often red or purple
  • Black and white: when the colour is ugly or the light is a mess of purple, black and white often saves the frame; see Black and white
  • Blacks: keep the background truly black; see Black and white points
  • Crop: to cut out stray microphone stands and the edge of the stage

Worked scenarios

  • A small pub gig, from the crowd. 50 mm at f/1.8, 1/250 s, auto ISO up to 6400. Spot meter on the singer's face. Wait for the warm wash of light, and shoot between the heads in front
  • A singer under red light. The face looks like a red blob. Lower exposure by two thirds of a stop, wait for a white or blue light, or plan to convert to black and white
  • A drummer at the back of a dark stage. 70-200 mm at f/2.8, 1/500 s, ISO 6400. Shoot from the side of the stage, through a gap in the cymbals, when the drummer looks up
  • A DJ at a club night, with flash allowed. 1/8 s at f/5.6, ISO 800, rear curtain sync, flash compensation minus 1. Twist the camera slightly as you press, so the lights swirl around a sharp DJ

Common mistakes

  • Shutter too slow. 1/60 s looks sharp on the back of the camera and blurred at home
  • Exposing for the black room. The faces burn out. Meter on the performer
  • Shooting non-stop. A thousand frames of nothing. Wait for the moment, then shoot a short burst
  • Forgetting earplugs

Your challenge

Go to a small local gig and ask the band or the venue if you may take photographs. Set 1/250 s, your widest aperture and auto ISO, and spot meter on the singer's face. Shoot no more than 100 frames, waiting for the light to hit a face each time. At home, pick your best three: one colour, one black and white, one of the crowd.
