Black and white points

The quickest way to make a flat photograph look finished: set the brightest tone to white and the darkest to black. Then use the same controls for punch or a soft, matte look.

Many photographs look slightly flat straight from the camera, as if seen through a thin grey veil. Often nothing is wrong with the exposure. The brightest part of the picture simply is not quite white, and the darkest part is not quite black.

Setting the white point and black point fixes that. It takes seconds, it does more than the Contrast slider, and it is one of the quickest ways to make a photograph look finished. It is also where some of the strongest looks start: deep and punchy, or soft and matte.

Starting values

  • Whites: +5 to +30 for most photographs, until the brightest important area just touches white
  • Blacks: minus 5 to minus 30, until the darkest important area just touches black
  • Clipping allowed: tiny specks on the sun, a street light or a reflection on water; never whole areas of cloud, snow or skin
  • For a soft, matte look: raise the black end of the Tone Curve so the darkest tone sits around 5 to 10 percent grey
  • Order: Exposure first, then Whites and Blacks, then Highlights and Shadows

These are starting points. Make the change, then step away and look again before you keep it.

What the black and white points are

Every photograph has a range of tones from its darkest pixel to its brightest. The white point is the level that becomes pure white, and the black point the level that becomes pure black. Everything in between is spread out between them.

When a photograph's tones stop short of both ends, it looks grey and low in contrast. Moving the ends out to meet white and black stretches the tones across the full range, so the picture gains depth and the colours look richer. The midtones barely move, which is why this often looks more natural than adding Contrast.

Reading the histogram

The histogram, at the top of Lightroom's Develop module, shows this at a glance. Shadows are on the left, highlights on the right.

  • A gap at both ends: the photograph is flat. Set both points
  • A tall spike pressed against the right edge: highlights are clipping, with detail lost to pure white
  • A tall spike against the left edge: shadows are clipping to pure black
  • A gap at one end only: a pale scene such as snow or mist, or a dark scene at night, may be meant to look that way

Press J to turn on the clipping warnings. Clipped highlights show red and clipped shadows show blue.

The fast way in Lightroom

In the Basic panel, Lightroom can set both points for you.

  1. Hold Shift and double-click the Whites slider. Lightroom moves it to about the point where clipping starts
  2. Hold Shift and double-click the Blacks slider. The same for the dark end
  3. Look at the result, and pull back either slider if it has gone too far

On many photographs that is the job done in under ten seconds. It is a better starting point than the Auto button, which changes several sliders at once.

The precise way: watch the clipping

For more control, hold Alt or Option while you drag the Whites or Blacks slider.

  • Whites: the picture turns black. Drag right until the first areas appear. White shows pure white with no detail; coloured specks show one colour clipping. Stop when the important areas just start to show
  • Blacks: the picture turns white. Drag left until the first dark areas appear, then stop

This shows exactly what is being lost, which the photograph itself hides at screen size. The demo above has the same views: switch to Show clipped whites or Show clipped blacks and move the sliders.

When to clip, and when not to

Pure white and pure black are not faults in themselves. What matters is what you lose.

  • Fine to clip: the sun, a street light, a bright reflection on water or metal, a small deep shadow under a rock
  • Avoid clipping: cloud with texture, snow, white buildings, skin, a white dress
  • Night and silhouettes: large areas of black are often right. The sky at night or a figure against a sunset can be pure black
  • Prints: most papers cannot show the very ends of the range. Leave a little room, especially in the shadows, and check with soft proofing; see Exporting for screen and print

Using the points for effect

The same two controls set the mood as well as fixing flatness.

  • Deep and punchy. Push Blacks a little further than needed so the darkest shadows go solid. Good for strong light, architecture and black and white
  • Soft and matte. In the Tone Curve, choose the point curve and drag the bottom-left point straight up a little. The darkest tone becomes a dark grey, giving the faded look of some film stock. Use it gently
  • High key. Push Whites so most of a pale scene sits near white, keeping a few darker details for shape. It suits snow, mist and minimal photographs
  • Low key. Let most of the frame fall to near black, with light picking out the subject

Photoshop and phones

In Photoshop, a Levels adjustment layer does the same job (Ctrl or Cmd L for a direct adjustment). Drag the black and white input sliders in towards the ends of the histogram, holding Alt or Option to see what clips. The black and white eyedroppers set the points by clicking the darkest and brightest areas.

Lightroom mobile has Whites and Blacks in its Light panel. Some phones' own editors have a Black Point slider that works in the same way.

Worked scenarios

  • A grey, hazy coast. The histogram has gaps at both ends. Shift and double-click the Whites slider (+22) and Blacks (minus 18). The rocks gain depth and the sea goes from grey to blue, with no Contrast needed
  • A snow scene. Shift and double-clicking the Whites slider clips the snow. Hold Alt or Option and pull Whites back until no large areas show white, around +10. Set Blacks only on the trees
  • A city at night. Blacks at minus 40 turns the sky solid black and hides noise. Keep it, but check that dark buildings keep their outline
  • A soft portrait. Set the points normally, then lift the black end of the Tone Curve slightly for a matte finish that flatters skin

Common mistakes

  • Using Contrast instead. Contrast pushes tones apart from the middle and can still leave the ends short of black and white
  • Clipping skies and snow. The detail does not come back on screen or in print
  • Crushing every shadow. A little detail in the darkest areas keeps the picture three-dimensional
  • Setting points before exposure. Fix the overall brightness first, or you will be resetting them again
  • A matte look on everything. Faded blacks stop looking like a choice when every picture has them

Checking your work

  • Press J for clipping warnings and look for red or blue in places that matter
  • Look at the histogram: the tones should reach close to both edges unless you chose otherwise
  • Press the backslash key to compare before and after
  • Look at it small as a thumbnail, where the gain in depth is easiest to see

Your challenge

Choose five photographs that look slightly flat. On each, hold Shift and double-click the Whites slider, then the Blacks slider, and note the values Lightroom chose. Then redo one by hand, holding Alt or Option as you drag, and compare. Finish one of the five with a gentle matte look from the Tone Curve and decide whether it suits it.
