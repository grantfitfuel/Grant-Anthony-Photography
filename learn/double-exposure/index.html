Double exposure

Two photographs in one frame: a silhouette filled with forest, a city inside a shape. How to make one in camera and in Photoshop.

The interactive version has a hands-on demo, a quiz, and keeps track of the lessons you have finished.

A double exposure lays one photograph over another in the same frame. On film, it happened when you took a second picture without winding on. Today most cameras have a multiple exposure mode, and you can also build one in editing. Done well, it makes a picture that neither photograph could be on its own: a face filled with forest, a city inside a silhouette, a tree growing through a building.

Starting settings

  • Camera mode: Multiple exposure, set to 2 exposures
  • Blending mode: Bright (also called Lighten) for silhouettes against a pale sky; Additive or Average for overlapping two scenes evenly
  • First frame, the base: a strong, simple shape against a bright, plain background, such as a person or tree against white sky
  • Exposure for the base: +1 to +2 stops on a plain sky, so the background goes white and the subject stays dark
  • Second frame, the texture: a detailed scene with plenty of light and dark, such as trees, flowers, water or city lights
  • Exposure in Additive mode: about minus 1 stop on each frame, so two frames add up to one normal exposure
  • Focus: manual, or check it on each frame. A soft texture is fine; a soft base is not
  • File format: RAW where the camera allows it, and keep the single frames if it offers to

These are starting points. Take a frame, check the histogram, and adjust one thing at a time.

How the camera blends the two

Different makers use different names, but most cameras offer these.

  • Bright, or Lighten: each part of the frame keeps whichever exposure is brighter there. Dark areas of the first frame fill with the second, and a white sky stays white. This is the classic silhouette double exposure
  • Dark, or Darken: each part keeps whichever exposure is darker. Dark shapes from both frames show
  • Additive: the light of both frames is added together, as on film. Easy to overexpose, so reduce each frame
  • Average: both frames at half strength, so the exposure looks after itself, but contrast can look weak

Use the demo above to compare them on two photographs.

The silhouette method

This is the look most people mean by double exposure.

  1. Find the base. A person in profile, a tree or a building against a plain, bright sky. Overcast sky is ideal
  2. Expose it bright. Meter on the sky and add one to two stops, so the sky turns white and the subject stays a dark silhouette
  3. Choose Bright or Lighten mode.
  4. Find the texture. Turn to a scene with detail: branches, leaves, a city skyline, waves. Many cameras show the first frame over the live view, so you can line up the second against it
  5. Place it. Put the detail where you want it inside the silhouette. Leave part of the subject's face or edge clear so the shape still reads
  6. Take the second frame and check the result. Move and try again; it rarely works first time

Other ideas

  • Two views of one city. A street scene and a skyline, in Average mode
  • Movement. A sharp frame plus a second with camera movement, for a soft echo; see Intentional camera movement
  • Flowers and texture. A flower sharp in one frame and out of focus in the other, for a soft glow
  • Self-portraits. Your own silhouette against a window, with a landscape you love inside it
  • Seasons. The same tree in two seasons, taken months apart, in editing

Double exposure in Photoshop

Editing gives more control and lets you combine photographs taken at different times. Lightroom Classic has no layers, so this is Photoshop work.

  1. Open the base photograph, then add the texture as a second layer (File > Place Embedded, or drag it in)
  2. Set the top layer's blend mode to Screen or Lighten. Screen brightens more smoothly; Lighten keeps whichever is lighter, like the camera's Bright mode
  3. Use Free Transform (Ctrl or Cmd T) to size and position the texture
  4. Add a layer mask and paint with a soft black brush to hide the texture where you do not want it, such as across the eyes
  5. Use a Levels or Curves layer to keep the background clean and white

Making it work

  • Keep the base simple. One clear shape. A busy base turns into mush
  • Keep the background plain and bright. Any detail in the sky shows through the texture
  • Contrast in the texture. A flat, grey second frame disappears
  • Leave something recognisable. An eye, a profile line, the top of a tree
  • Convert to black and white if the colours fight

Honesty

A double exposure is obviously a made photograph, which is part of its appeal. Still, say how you made it when it matters: some competitions want in-camera double exposures only, and some allow none. See Composites and sky replacement for more on when combined photographs are acceptable.

Worked scenarios

  • A portrait in profile against an overcast sky. Meter the sky, add 1.7 stops, and the face becomes a dark silhouette on white. Second frame: autumn branches, in Bright mode, placed so the branches fill the back of the head and the face edge stays clean
  • A tree at blue hour. A bare tree silhouetted at dusk, then a city's lights out of focus as the second frame in Additive mode, each at minus 1 stop
  • Two frames of waves. One at 1/500 s, one at 1 s, in Average mode, for sharp spray over smooth water

Common mistakes

  • Two busy frames. Detail on detail and nothing reads. Make one frame simple
  • A grey background. The texture shows everywhere. Overexpose the base sky until it is white
  • Overexposing in Additive mode. Take a stop off each frame
  • Covering the face entirely. Leave the eye or the profile clear

Checking your result

  • Can you still tell what the subject is from across the room?
  • Is the background clean, or is the texture leaking into it?
  • Does the texture follow the shape, or fight it?
  • Look at it small, as it will appear on a phone

Your challenge

Make one silhouette double exposure in camera. Ask someone to stand in profile against an overcast sky, or use a tree. Expose the sky bright so it turns white, set Bright or Lighten mode, then add a second frame of branches or leaves. Take at least ten attempts, moving the second frame each time, and keep the one where the shape still reads clearly.
