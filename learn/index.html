Light

  • The exposure triangleAperture, shutter speed and ISO decide how bright a photograph is, and each one changes how it looks. Move the controls and see for yourself.
  • Reading the lightThe same hill looks completely different at seven in the morning and two in the afternoon. Learn to see the light before you look for a subject.
  • White balanceLight has a colour, even when it looks white. What white balance does, which setting to use when, and the kelvin values behind them.
  • Night and blue hour in citiesCities come alive after sunset. For a short time the sky and the street lights balance perfectly, and that window is worth planning around.
  • Shooting into the sunTurn round and face the light. Silhouettes, sunstars and glowing edges, and how to expose them without flare, blown skies or harm to your eyes.
  • Planning a photography tripGood light is mostly luck, but you can tip the odds. Time, weather and a bit of homework put you in the right place when it happens.

Composition

  • The rule of thirdsSplit the frame into nine and put what matters where the lines cross. The simplest way to stop every picture looking like a snapshot.
  • Where to put the horizonHigh, low or dead centre. Where the horizon sits decides whether a photograph is about the land or the sky.
  • Leading linesRoads, walls, rivers and shorelines can walk the eye straight to your subject. Find the line first, then the point it leads to.
  • Framing within the frameAn archway, a window or a gap in the trees puts a border inside your photograph and holds the eye on what matters.
  • Symmetry and patternsReflections, repeating shapes and perfect balance. When to centre everything, and why breaking a pattern is often the photograph.
  • The golden ratioThe spiral and the phi grid, where they come from, and how much weight to give them.
  • Composing a landscapeA wide view rarely works on its own. Give the eye a way in, a place to rest and a reason to stay.
  • MinimalismOne subject, a lot of empty space and nothing else. Minimalism is the art of taking things out until the picture breaks, then putting one back.
  • ReflectionsStill water, puddles, wet sand and glass give you the same subject twice. Get low, wait for calm, and focus on the reflection, not the surface.

Technique

  • Telephoto landscapesLandscapes are not only for wide-angle lenses. A long lens picks one shape out of a big view and stacks distant layers on top of each other.
  • Long exposureLeave the shutter open for seconds or minutes and the moving world disappears. Water turns to mist, clouds to streaks, traffic to ribbons of light.
  • FiltersMost effects can be added later in editing. Two cannot: a polariser removing glare, and an ND filter slowing the shutter. Those are the filters worth carrying.
  • Black and whiteTake the colour away and a photograph is only light, shadow, shape and texture. Some pictures get stronger. Some fall apart.
  • Intentional camera movementMove the camera during a long exposure on purpose and a wood becomes a painting. Easy to try, hard to master, and impossible to repeat exactly.
  • Double exposureTwo photographs in one frame: a silhouette filled with forest, a city inside a shape. How to make one in camera and in Photoshop.

Editing

  • Organising and backing upImport with care, cull quickly, label what you keep, and hold three copies of every photograph on three different storage points, one of them away from home.
  • Editing lightlyEditing is not cheating, and it does not have to take hours. A few small adjustments bring a photograph back to what you saw.
  • Black and white pointsThe quickest way to make a flat photograph look finished: set the brightest tone to white and the darkest to black. Then use the same controls for punch or a soft, matte look.
  • Cleaning up a photographSensor dust, distractions, colour fringes and leaning lines. How to fix them in Lightroom and Photoshop so nobody notices, and when you should not remove anything at all.
  • Sharpening and noiseWhere noise comes from, how to remove it with Denoise or the sliders, how to sharpen without halos, and the order to do it in.
  • Editing effectsGlow, dodging and burning, vignettes, colour grading and grain. Optional effects that can lift the right photograph, as long as nobody notices them.
  • Taking it too farEvery slider looks good for the first few notches. What happens when you keep going, how to spot it, and how to pull it back.
  • Cropping and aspect ratiosThe shape of the frame changes what a photograph is about. Choose it in camera where you can, crop with care, and know what each crop costs in pixels.
  • Presets and a consistent lookA preset saves slider settings so a set of photographs starts from the same place. It is a starting point, not a finished edit.
  • Exporting for screen and printThe same edit can look right on one screen and wrong everywhere else. Choose the size, colour space and sharpening for where the photograph is going.
  • Monitor calibrationIf your screen is too bright or too blue, every edit corrects a fault that is not in the photograph. Set the screen right before you trust it.
  • Editing on a phoneThe editor on your phone and the Lightroom mobile app can finish most pictures. Edit in the shade, at half brightness, and save at full size.
  • Portrait retouchingLight, respectful retouching that keeps skin looking like skin. Remove what is temporary, keep what is theirs, and ask first.
  • Composites and sky replacementBlending frames from one moment is one thing. Dropping in a sky from another day is another. How to match the light, and how to be straight about it.

Genres

  • ArchitectureBuildings do not move and the light on them is predictable, which makes architecture the most patient subject there is. The hard part is the lines.
  • StreetCandid moments of everyday life in public places. Be ready before the moment arrives, and treat the people in your pictures with respect.
  • WoodlandA wood is the hardest landscape to photograph well. The trick is finding order in the chaos, and the right weather does half of it for you.
  • SeascapesWaves, tides and wet rock. Read the sea before you shoot it, time the picture to the water, and keep yourself and your kit safe from it.
  • AbstractTake away the context and a photograph stops being of something. It becomes shape, line, colour and texture. Composition matters more, not less.
  • City breaksTwo or three days, a lot of famous places and no time to wait for the light. Travel light, plan around the sun, and look beyond the postcard.
  • Music and gigsDim, coloured, ever-changing light and performers who never stand still. The settings, timing and manners that make gig photography work.
  • Found detailsStreet art, signs, worn surfaces and the small things everyone walks past. Slow down, get close, and let a detail carry the whole picture.
