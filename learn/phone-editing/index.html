Editing on a phone

The editor on your phone and the Lightroom mobile app can finish most pictures. Edit in the shade, at half brightness, and save at full size.

The interactive version has a quiz, and keeps track of the lessons you have finished.

Open the interactive lesson

A phone is often the camera you have with you, and its editor is often the quickest way to finish a picture. For many photographs it is all you need. My own editing is light, mainly in Lightroom, and the same light approach works on a phone.

The tools are smaller and the screen is less reliable, but the order of work is the same as on a computer. Fix the shape, set the light, then the colour, then stop.

Starting values

  • Straighten: within 1 or 2 degrees of level for most horizons; check against the grid overlay
  • Exposure or Brightness: plus or minus 0.3 to 0.7 in Lightroom mobile; small nudges in a built-in editor
  • Highlights: minus 20 to minus 50 for a bright sky
  • Shadows: +10 to +40 to open dark areas without turning them grey
  • Warmth or Temp: small moves either side of the starting point; on a RAW file in Lightroom mobile, a few hundred kelvin at most, and on other files a few points
  • Vibrance: +5 to +20, gentler than Saturation
  • Screen brightness while editing: around half, with automatic brightness and night modes off

These are starting points. Make the change, then step away and look again before you keep it.

The phone's built-in editor

Every phone has an editor in its photo gallery. The names vary, but most offer the same core tools:

  • Crop and straighten. Level the horizon first, then crop. Many editors also correct tilt in two directions, which helps with buildings
  • Exposure or brightness. The overall lightness of the picture
  • Highlights and shadows. Recover a bright sky; open up dark areas
  • Contrast. The difference between light and dark
  • Warmth. Cooler (bluer) or warmer (more yellow)
  • Saturation or vibrance. The strength of the colours

On most phones these edits are reversible. The editor keeps the original and stores your changes alongside it, so you can go back to the original later. Check your own phone for a revert or undo option before you rely on it, and be careful with anything that saves a copy over the original or deletes the first version.

In most editors, pressing and holding on the picture shows the original for as long as you hold. Use it often. It is the phone version of a before and after view.

The Lightroom mobile app

The Lightroom mobile app gives you more control, laid out much like Lightroom Classic. The editing tools are grouped into panels:

  • Light: Exposure, Contrast, Highlights, Shadows, Whites and Blacks, plus a curve
  • Color: white balance with Temp and Tint, Vibrance, Saturation, and a colour mixer for individual colours
  • Effects: Texture, Clarity, Dehaze, Vignette and Grain
  • Detail: sharpening and noise reduction

It also has Masking, with Select Subject, Select Sky, a brush and linear and radial gradients. A mask lets you darken a sky or lighten a face without touching the rest of the picture, exactly as on a computer.

Lightroom mobile can also show a histogram while you edit. Turn it on. The histogram does not depend on how bright your screen is, so it is the honest check.

Syncing with Lightroom Classic

Photos edited in the mobile app do not appear in Lightroom Classic by themselves. The two only share photos and edits through Lightroom's cloud sync, and only when you have set it up: in Lightroom Classic you choose which collections to sync, and photos added on the phone arrive in Classic through that sync.

If you have not set this up, treat the phone and the computer as separate. Export the finished file from the phone and keep it with the rest of your photographs. Backing up is covered in Organising and backing up.

Shooting RAW on a phone

Many phones offer a RAW option in the camera app, often saving a DNG file. A RAW file keeps more of what the sensor recorded, so you have more room to recover a bright sky, open shadows and change white balance without the picture breaking up.

It helps most when:

  • The light is contrasty, such as a bright sky over a dark street
  • White balance is tricky, such as mixed street lighting
  • You plan to edit properly in Lightroom mobile or on a computer

It helps less than you might expect in other cases. A phone normally merges several frames and applies its own processing to make the everyday picture, and a plain RAW file may skip some of that. In dim light, the phone's standard picture can look cleaner than a RAW file you edit yourself. RAW files are also several times larger, so they fill storage faster. Try both on the same scene and compare.

The limits of a phone screen

A phone screen is small, often very bright, and usually set to change brightness by itself. All three mislead you:

  • Small. Fine detail, noise and halos are hard to see. Zoom in to check edges
  • Bright. A bright screen makes every picture look brighter than it is, so you tend to edit too dark. The same picture looks murky on other screens
  • Automatic brightness and night modes. The screen changes as you move, and a night mode tints everything warm. Your colour decisions follow the tint

Turn off automatic brightness and any night or reading mode while you edit, and set the screen to around half. More on screens is in Monitor calibration.

Where you edit

Light around you changes what you see. In bright sun, you turn the screen up and still cannot judge the picture. Find shade, or wait until you are indoors. Avoid editing under a strongly coloured light, which pulls your sense of colour with it.

A useful habit for travellers: do a quick edit to share on the day, and a careful one later, on a larger screen, from the original.

Exporting and sharing

When you save or share an edited picture, check the size. Many apps offer a smaller size by default to save space. In Lightroom mobile, choose full size in the export options so you keep every pixel. You can always make a smaller copy later; you cannot add pixels back.

To post to Instagram, share straight from the editor or the app, or upload the saved file. Instagram resizes photographs to 1080 pixels wide, so a full size file is more than enough. Instagram also has its own filters and adjustments. If you have already edited the picture, leave them alone, or the edits stack. Sizes and shapes for the web are covered in Exporting for screen and print.

Worked scenarios

  • A harbour at midday. The sky is white and the boats are dark. Straighten the horizon by 1.5 degrees, set Highlights to minus 45 to bring back the sky, Shadows to +30 for the hulls, and Vibrance to +10. Press and hold to compare with the original
  • A street at dusk, shot in RAW. Mixed light leaves faces orange. In Lightroom mobile, lower Temp by about 400 K, then use Select Sky and lower its Exposure by 0.3 so the blue holds. Check the histogram, not the screen
  • A quick share on the move. Crop to the shape you want, nudge Exposure up by 0.3 in the built-in editor, save at full size and post. Edit it properly that evening from the original

Common mistakes

  • Editing in bright sun at full brightness. Pictures look dark and heavy everywhere else. Edit in shade with the screen at around half
  • Leaving night mode on. Everything is edited to look right through a warm tint, so it looks cold on other screens. Turn it off while you edit
  • Too much at once. Phone sliders are sensitive and a small swipe goes a long way. Make small moves and compare
  • Stacking filters. An edit, then an Instagram filter on top. Choose one
  • Exporting small. A shrunken copy is all you have left. Save at full size

Checking your work

  • Press and hold to see the original, or use the before and after view
  • Check the histogram for a sky pushed against the right edge or shadows crushed against the left
  • Zoom in to check edges, faces and skies for halos and noise
  • Look at the picture on a second screen, such as a computer or a tablet, before you print it

Your challenge

Take one photograph on your phone of a scene with a bright sky and dark foreground. If your phone offers RAW, take a second frame in RAW. Edit both, in the shade with the screen at around half and night mode off, using only straighten, exposure, highlights, shadows and warmth. Save at full size and compare them on a larger screen the next day.
