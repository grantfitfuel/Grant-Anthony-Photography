Taking it too far

Every slider looks good for the first few notches. What happens when you keep going, how to spot it, and how to pull it back.

Every slider in Lightroom goes to 100, and every one of them looks good for the first few notches. That is the trap. Each move looks like an improvement on the one before, so you keep going, and an hour later the photograph is louder than the place ever was.

This lesson goes slider by slider through what happens when you push too far, how to spot it, and how to pull it back. My own editing is light, so take this as one view. The test is not how much you change. It is whether the photograph still looks like light falling on a real place.

Starting values

These are points where trouble often starts on a typical landscape or city photograph. They are not limits; a misty morning or a flat grey day may need more.

  • Saturation: 0 to +10, and prefer Vibrance at +5 to +20 first
  • Shadows: up to about +50; past that, check for grey and noise
  • Highlights: down to about minus 70; past that, bright areas turn flat and dull
  • Clarity: +5 to +25 on landscapes; 0 or below on faces
  • Dehaze: +5 to +20; past +30, skies darken fast
  • Texture: +5 to +25
  • Sharpening Amount: 30 to 60 on a RAW file, with Masking raised to protect skies
  • Vignette: minus 5 to minus 20

These are starting points. Make the change, then step away and look again before you keep it.

Why it happens

Your eyes adapt. After a few minutes looking at a warm, saturated photograph, it starts to look normal, so the next push seems needed. The same thing happens with contrast and sharpness.

The slider also gives you a fresh comparison at every step: each position is judged against the one before, not against the scene. And a bright screen in a dark room makes everything look punchier than it will on anyone else's phone.

Saturation and vibrance

Too much saturation does more than make colours loud. Strong colours run out of room first, so a red flower, an orange sunset or a blue sign turns into a flat patch with no detail inside it. Lightroom's histogram shows it: a colour spike pressed against one edge.

Vibrance is gentler because it lifts muted colours more than strong ones, but it can be overdone too. Skies turn electric and grass looks like plastic. If one colour is the problem, use the HSL/Color panel and adjust that colour alone.

Shadows and highlights

Pulling Highlights right down and pushing Shadows right up is the quickest route to the flat, grey "HDR look". Light only looks like light because of the shade around it. Lift every shadow and the photograph loses depth and the sense of time of day.

Lifted shadows also show noise, because the dark parts of a file hold the least information. If you need to lift a lot, use a mask on just the area that needs it, and set the Blacks slider so something in the frame is still truly dark.

Clarity, Texture and Dehaze

These three add contrast in different places, and all three draw attention to themselves when pushed.

  • Clarity adds midtone contrast over a wide radius. Too much draws a pale or dark outline wherever a dark edge meets a bright one, such as a hill against the sky
  • Texture works on fine detail. Too much makes everything look gritty, and skin looks rough
  • Dehaze cuts haze by adding contrast and colour. Too much darkens the sky, turns blue skies navy, adds noise and can darken the corners

Use the demo above to see each one fall over. Press All of them and watch how several "reasonable" pushes add up.

Sharpening and noise reduction

Heavy sharpening leaves thin halos along edges and turns smooth skies to grit. Heavy noise reduction does the opposite: stone, bark and skin turn to wax. Both show at 100 percent, not at screen size, so check them there. The Sharpening and noise lesson covers the settings in detail.

Colour and white balance

Overcooked colour is often a white balance problem. A sunset warmed by a further 1000 K looks fiery on screen and orange everywhere else. Look at something that should be neutral, such as white paint, grey stone or clouds away from the sun. If it has turned orange or blue, you have gone too far.

Colour grading is easy to overdo for the same reason: your eye gets used to the tint. Keep the saturation on each wheel low and switch the panel off now and then to see the difference.

Masks and vignettes

Masks make it easy to push one area further than the rest. Look for the edges: a glowing outline round a tree line after a sky mask, a bright patch on a face, a radial filter that shows as a halo. Lower the exposure or saturation inside the mask until the join disappears.

A vignette should go unnoticed. If you can see a dark ring before you look for it, halve it.

Worked scenarios

  • A sunset over the sea. Saturation +40 and Vibrance +40 made the sky solid orange with no gradation. Reset Saturation to 0, Vibrance to +15, and lower the orange saturation in HSL by 10. The colour stays strong and the detail returns
  • A woodland on a dull day. Shadows +100, Highlights minus 100 and Clarity +60 gave every leaf an outline and made the whole frame grey. Shadows +40, Highlights minus 40, Clarity +15 and Blacks minus 10 brings back depth
  • A city at blue hour. Dehaze +50 turned the sky nearly black and added noise. Dehaze +10, plus a small exposure lift on the sky with a mask, keeps the blue

Common mistakes

  • Editing in a dark room on a bright screen. Everything looks punchier than it is. Turn the brightness down to around half
  • Judging at one size only. Halos and grit show at 100 percent; heavy colour and contrast show as a thumbnail. Check both
  • Using a bought preset at full strength. Most are made to stand out. Set the preset's Amount to 50 and build from there
  • Fixing one overdone slider with another. Too much Dehaze, then Shadows to fix the dark sky, then Saturation to fix the grey. Go back to the first slider instead
  • Editing for the thumbnail. Loud photographs stand out in a feed, but they tire the eye in a print on the wall

Checking your edit

  • Press the backslash key in the Develop module to flip between before and after, or Y to see them side by side
  • Use the History panel to step back through your changes and see where it started going wrong
  • Halve your last adjustment and compare. If you cannot tell which is which, keep the smaller one
  • Double-click a slider's name to reset it to zero
  • Hold Alt or Option while dragging Whites or Blacks to see what is clipping
  • Look at the photograph the next morning, on your phone as well as your computer

A strong sky and warm sand already carry plenty of colour and contrast. Extra Dehaze or Saturation here would turn the storm black and the dunes to flat orange.

Your challenge

Choose a photograph you edited a while ago. Find its three strongest adjustments, in the sliders or the History panel, and halve each one. Compare the two versions with the backslash key, then look at both on your phone the next morning. Keep the one that looks more like the place.
